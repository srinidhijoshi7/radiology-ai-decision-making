Case study: chest X-ray AI as a decision partner

A condensed narrative of the regulatory, technical and behavioural arc of diagnostic AI in radiology — from IDx-DR's 2018 FDA milestone to the bias audits that reshaped how the field thinks about deployment.

The regulatory milestone: IDx-DR (2018)

On 11 April 2018 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration granted De Novo authorisation to IDx-DR, the first medical device permitted to make an autonomous diagnosis without physician interpretation (FDA, 2018). The device screens fundus photographs for diabetic retinopathy and returns one of two binary recommendations: refer to an ophthalmologist, or rescreen in twelve months. The pivotal trial reported sensitivity of 87.4% and specificity of 89.5% across 900 patients in 10 primary care sites (Abràmoff et al., 2018, npj Digital Medicine).

IDx-DR is significant less for its accuracy than for its decision architecture: it bypasses the human reader entirely on negative cases, removing both the cost of a referral and the cognitive interaction effects we study elsewhere on this site. It is the ceiling of what "human-out-of-the-loop" looks like in regulated radiology. Most deployed systems sit closer to the floor.

The everyday case: CheXpert and the chest radiograph reader

The dominant clinical AI workflow today is assistive: a convolutional model produces a per-pathology probability and surfaces flagged studies for the radiologist to confirm or override. Stanford's CheXpert (Irvin et al., 2019) is the canonical academic example, with a DenseNet-121 trained on 224,316 chest radiographs achieving radiologist-level AUC on five conditions (atelectasis, cardiomegaly, consolidation, edema, pleural effusion).

Commercial counterparts — Aidoc, Annalise.ai, Lunit INSIGHT CXR — are now reimbursable in several jurisdictions and read tens of millions of studies annually. The behavioural design choice that defines them is presentation order: do you flash the AI's prediction on the worklist before the radiologist opens the image, or do you reveal it only after they've recorded their own read?

The behavioural finding: anchoring is real

Two experimental papers form the empirical backbone of our analysis. Tschandl et al. (2020, Nature Medicine) showed dermatology AI suggestions to 302 dermatologists and found that experts with AI assistance gained accuracy when AI was correct (+13.0 pp) but lost accuracy when AI was wrong (−11.3 pp), with the loss compounding when the suggestion was shown before the clinician's own read.

Gaube et al. (2021, npj Digital Medicine) replicated the effect for chest X-rays: 265 clinicians evaluating eight cases each, randomised to receive AI suggestions before or after their independent diagnosis. Prior-suggestion clinicians were significantly more likely to accept incorrect AI advice — a 4.4 pp accuracy gap purely attributable to ordering, not to the AI itself. We borrow Gaube's estimated deference weight (wAI ≈ 0.45) as the empirical anchor in our simulator.

The fairness wrinkle

Seyyed-Kalantari et al. (2021, Nature Medicine) audited three publicly released chest X-ray classifiers (including Stanford's CheXpert release) and found systematic under-diagnosis of female, Black, Hispanic and low-socioeconomic patients — the AI was more likely to label these patients as "no finding" even when a pathology was present. Under-diagnosis is the failure mode that most directly compounds with anchoring: an under-diagnosing AI shown first nudges a clinician toward false reassurance precisely on the patients who most need a careful read.

What our analysis adds

  • A reproducible 234-study CheXpert-style sample with published-AUC-calibrated AI predictions and Seyyed-Kalantari-magnitude subgroup gaps.
  • A Bayesian belief-update model that turns Gaube's deference weight into a continuous, sweepable parameter — visible in the interactive simulator.
  • A demonstration that in this dataset, the AI-shown-first protocol raises overall accuracy by 2.9 pp but drops accuracy on AI-wrong cases by 6.1 pp — the empirical price of the anchoring hand-off.

Lessons for analytics design

The headline result is that workflow ordering is a behavioural variable as consequential as the model itself. Three takeaways that generalise beyond radiology:

  1. Default to AI-after. Surface the model's prediction only once the user has committed to their independent judgement, even if the average accuracy gain is smaller. Safety on the long tail of AI errors matters more than mean performance.
  2. Calibrate confidence, not just discrimination. Brier scores and reliability diagrams should be standard reporting. Over-confident models invite over-deference.
  3. Audit subgroup performance before deployment. Anchoring on a biased model is the failure mode that combines automation bias with algorithmic discrimination — a compound risk no single discipline owns.
