What this project is

Diagnostic AI is increasingly embedded in clinical workflows: the Stanford CheXpert and CheXNet models for chest X-ray, IDx-DR for diabetic retinopathy, and a growing number of FDA-cleared radiology tools. The technical story is well-rehearsed — AUC numbers, headline accuracy gains, regulatory milestones. The behavioural story is murkier.

This project asks: how does the way AI advice is delivered change the human decision? Drawing on Kahneman's dual-process theory, Logg et al.'s algorithm appreciation findings and Gaube et al.'s 2021 anchoring experiments, we run an empirical simulation showing the cognitive cost of getting the human-AI hand-off wrong.

How I built it

1. Calibrated dataset

A 234-study CheXpert-style validation sample with AI predictions whose per-pathology AUCs match Irvin et al. (2019), and demographic-bias gaps modelled on Seyyed-Kalantari et al. (2021). Source CSV in data/chexpert_sample.csv.

2. Statistical audit

ROC, calibration / Brier scores, Cohen's κ for human-AI agreement, and disparity metrics across patient sex and age. Reproducible via python src/analysis.py.

3. Anchoring simulator

A Bayesian log-odds model of the radiologist's posterior under three AI presentation protocols. The interactive simulator lets you sweep the anchoring weight and watch accuracy unravel when the AI is wrong.

Why it matters

The dominant narrative around medical AI is "human-in-the-loop" — that a clinician's judgement will catch the model's mistakes. Our results, consistent with the experimental literature, suggest the opposite can hold true under standard deployment: when AI is shown first, clinicians accept its errors at a measurable rate, and overall safety can degrade even as average accuracy improves. The implication for analytics-led decision-design is that workflow ordering is a behavioural variable as consequential as the model itself.
